Introduction

The growing concern over space debris has led to increased efforts to ensure that satellites are responsibly deorbited at the end of their operational life. According to ESA’s “Space Debris Mitigation Requirement”, if the spacecraft relies on natural orbital decay for deorbiting it has to deorbit in 5 years or less

This mitigates the risk of collisions but also helps maintain a sustainable orbital environment. One critical aspect of this process is understanding how atmospheric drag influences the deorbiting timeline for satellites in Low-Earth Orbit (LEO).

In this project, GMAT (General Mission Analysis Tool) is used to simulate the deorbiting process of a satellite under the influence of atmospheric drag. The results are then analysed with Python to visualise key orbital parameters over time, such as altitude, eccentricity, and semi-major axis.

This blog post will walk you through the methodology, results, and implications of our analysis, offering insights into how engineers and scientists can better prepare for the challenges of operating in an increasingly crowded orbital environment.

Methodology

Step 1: Setting up the GMAT Simulation

Begin by configuring a spacecraft (Sat1) in GMAT with the following initial parameters:

  • Epoch: January 1, 2025
  • Initial Position (X, Y, Z): (4.211560342267748e-13, 6878 km, 0)
  • Initial Velocity (VX, VY, VZ): (0.9541223000883489, -5.842314103991534e-17, 7.552655699128404)
  • Dry Mass: 3 kg
  • Drag Coefficient (Cd): 2.2
  • Cross-sectional Area for Drag: 0.01281 m²
  • Solar Radiation Pressure Area (SRP): 0.01 m²

Additionally, configure the force model to include Earth’s gravity (degree and order 4), atmospheric drag using the MSISE90 model, and other environmental factors such as solar flux (F10.7 = 150) and geomagnetic activity (Magnetic Index = 3).

The propagator is set up with a Runge-Kutta 89 integrator, ensuring high accuracy during the simulation. The propagation continues until the satellite reaches a reentry altitude of 120 km.

Step 2: Generating and Analysing Data

After running the simulation in GMAT, the output data is saved in a report file (e.g., AltitudeReport.txt). This file contains detailed information about the satellite’s orbital parameters over time, including:

  • Altitude
  • Elapsed Days
  • True Anomaly (TA)
  • Eccentricity (ECC)
  • Argument of Perigee (AOP)
  • Semi-Major Axis (SMA)
  • Inclination (INC)
  • Right Ascension of the Ascending Node (RAAN)

Results

1. Satellite Altitude vs Time

The first plot shows the satellite’s altitude decreasing over time due to atmospheric drag. The simulation indicates that the satellite takes approximately 5.01 years to deorbit from its initial altitude of 500 km to the reentry altitude of 120 km.

2. True Anomaly vs Time

The true anomaly (TA) represents the angle between the satellite’s position and its perigee. Over time, the true anomaly oscillates, reflecting the satellite’s orbit evolution under the influence of drag.

3. Eccentricity vs Time

The eccentricity of the orbit gradually increases as the satellite descends, indicating that the orbit becomes more elliptical due to atmospheric drag.

4. Semi-Major Axis vs Time

The semi-major axis decreases steadily over time, showing the shrinking orbit as the satellite loses energy due to drag.

5. Inclination and RAAN vs Time

The inclination and right ascension of the ascending node (RAAN) remain relatively stable throughout the simulation, as these parameters are less affected by atmospheric drag.

Discussion

The simulation results highlight the significant impact of atmospheric drag on satellite orbits in LEO. Key observations include:

  • The satellite’s altitude decreases steadily, leading to reentry within approximately 5 years.
  • The orbit becomes more elliptical over time, as indicated by the increasing eccentricity.
  • Parameters like inclination and RAAN remain largely unaffected, demonstrating their relative insensitivity to drag.

These findings underscore the importance of designing satellites with appropriate deorbiting strategies, especially as the number of objects in LEO continues to grow.

Conclusion

With GMAT for orbital mechanics simulations and Python for data analysis, the results provide valuable insights into the dynamics of atmospheric drag and its effects on satellite orbits. Such analyses are crucial for ensuring the long-term sustainability of space operations and mitigating the risks posed by space debris.

Future work could explore additional factors such as solar activity variations, different satellite configurations, and alternative deorbiting technologies like propulsion systems or tethers.
